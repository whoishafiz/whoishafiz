  • PhD, Systems EngineeringUniversiti Brunei Darussalam · Jan 2023 – presentResearch overview · timeline

    Lithium-ion batteries fade with every charge cycle. Knowing a cell's state of health (SoH) and how many cycles it has left, its remaining useful life (RUL), lets equipment be serviced before it fails rather than after. My PhD develops deep learning models that predict both from a cell's cycling history, and the data and evaluation practices that make those predictions trustworthy.

    Each cell is charged and discharged until it wears out, with voltage, current and temperature logged throughout. The charge a cell delivers in one discharge, compared with when it was new, gives its state of health for that cycle. I work with public datasets from NASA PCoE, the University of Bologna and CALCE (University of Maryland).

    Before any model sees the data, the logged values are screened. In many natural measurements, how often each leading digit appears follows Benford's law. Logged data that drifts from its expected digit pattern points to outliers or logging errors, which are flagged before training. This step is published (J. Phys.: Conf. Ser. 2024).

    Data are split before anything is scaled or tuned, and scalers are fitted on training data only. A model tested on late cycles of cells it trained on has already seen how those cells behave, which inflates its accuracy. Holding out whole cells measures how well it predicts a battery it has never met.

    The model reads a sliding window of a cell's most recent cycles and estimates where the cell stands. Recurrent networks (LSTM and GRU) suit this because they carry information along the sequence. Architectures and hyperparameters are tuned systematically, and every configuration is trained several times over different random seeds so that results do not hinge on one lucky run.

    The two outputs answer different maintenance questions. State of health says how worn a cell is now; remaining useful life says how many cycles it has before it reaches end of life, usually set at 70–80% of its original capacity. Cells age at different rates, which is why a model must be judged on cells it has not seen.

    Thesis · Solar PV cooling for tropical climates

    A solar panel converts less of its light into electricity as its cells heat up, and in the tropics an uncooled panel can reach 85 °C. My MSc thesis compared ways of keeping panels cool under Brunei Darussalam's climate. I modelled a stand-alone panel and three cooled designs in SOLIDWORKS, simulated their heat transfer in ANSYS against local weather data, and measured how much temperature and power each cooling method recovers.

    The simulations run on Brunei Darussalam's weather. Hourly solar radiation comes from five years of Brunei Darussalam Meteorological Services data (Mathew et al., 2013); diffuse radiation is estimated from it, and hourly air temperature and wind come from 2019 records. April (warmest, peak sunshine) and November (coolest) bracket the year.

    The panel is a LONGi LR6-60HPH (330 W, half-cut cells), so only one half (811.5 × 935.5 mm) needs to be simulated. It was drawn in SOLIDWORKS and its heat flow solved in ANSYS. Run at 35 °C and 500 W/m², the model gave 60.3 °C maximum and 59.9 °C minimum, in line with Hudişteanu et al. (2015), which validated it for the transient tropical runs.

    Three cooling methods suited to the tropics were added to the same panel. Air cooling uses the wind over a canal or open water flowing through a channel behind the panel. Water cooling trickles a thin film over the glass (0.0556 kg/s at 0.1 m/s, entering at 25 °C). The phase-change design fits two finned aluminium containers of paraffin wax behind the panel, leaving room for its junction box; the wax absorbs heat as it melts.

    Without cooling the panel peaked at 85.0 °C in April. Air and water cooling brought it down by 12–14 °C, and the phase-change design by 24 °C (28.5%), the most of any method. The wax only melts above about 58 °C, so it absorbed its latent heat in April but not in cooler November.

    Hotter cells convert less light: about 0.45% less efficiency for every degree above 25 °C. Applying that to each simulated day gives the average power per panel. The phase-change design delivered 6.3–7.7% more than the uncooled panel, roughly twice the gain of air (2.7–3.4%) or water cooling (3.5–4.3%).

    Dissertation · Direct-drive wave energy converter

    A point absorber is a buoy that heaves with the waves and drives a generator. In a direct-drive design the buoy connects straight to a linear generator, without gearboxes or chains to lose energy, but the generator's reaction force must be controlled for the device to perform well and survive rough seas. My dissertation built a simulation of such a device in MATLAB Simulink to size it and test control schemes before anything is built.

    A buoy on a spring of water, slowed by damping, moves like current in a series RLC circuit. Rewriting the device this way lets it be built and tested in MATLAB Simulink with standard electrical blocks. The buoy's mass, damping and stiffness become L, R and C, and the direct-drive generator becomes an RC branch whose back EMF is the force it exerts on the buoy.

    The linear model was checked step by step: free oscillation, a step input, varying wave amplitude, then sweeping wave frequency. The sweep showed a clear resonance peak, the frequency at which the buoy moves most and harvests most energy. A second, smaller buoy (10 t plus 7.9 t of added water mass, 2.75 m tall) resonated higher, as a smaller device should.

    At resonance an unrestrained buoy moves violently, which risks damaging it. The non-linear models add a switch that raises the generator's damping when motion gets too large. The revised condition compares the buoy's position with the wave level: if the water leaves a window the height of the buoy, the stronger damping engages. Below about 1.6 rad/s the switch stays dormant and the device behaves exactly like the linear model.

    Off resonance (1.3 rad/s) every version produced about 20 kW peaks, because the switch never engaged. At resonance the uncontrolled model peaked near 400 kW, but only by letting the buoy move without limit. The first switch model produced force spikes that showed a flawed condition. The revised model gave a smooth, stable 80 kW at 8,000 N·s/m; doubling the damping cost power and distorted the output, pointing to an optimum damping value. Sizing the buoy to resonate with local waves sets how much energy is on offer; control decides how much can be taken safely.
